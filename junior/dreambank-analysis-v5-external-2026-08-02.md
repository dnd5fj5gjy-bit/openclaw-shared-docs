# Dream Bank - our read on the model

**2 August 2026.** Bear Grylls Ventures. Based on the financial model circulated by Jack on 31 July,
and Companies House records checked 2 August 2026.

---

Thank you for sending the model. It is a better document than most we see at this stage: it labels its
own guesses, it raises the hardest questions against itself, and it documents an error an earlier
version made and corrected. That is rare and it is worth saying first.

What follows is our honest read. It is written to be useful rather than polite, and every figure in it
comes from your own sheet.

---

## The structural point: you are renting the bank

ClearBank holds the licence, the accounts and the money. Dream Bank owns the app and the brand. That is
a sensible way to start and it avoids years of regulatory work. But it makes ClearBank a landlord
rather than a supplier, and the rent is the largest number in the plan.

ClearBank takes 35bps off the base rate before Dream Bank sees any interest, then invoices separately
for the platform, for every account above 5,000, for every payment and for every identity check. Over
the three modelled years the deposit book generates roughly **£20.0m of gross interest**. Of that,
**£11.2m, or 56%, ends up with ClearBank** - £1.9m in retained margin and £9.3m in fees including the
£180k implementation charge. Dream Bank keeps the rest and pays its staff, its offices and its
marketing out of it.

In Year 1 the payment fees alone are **£840k against £797k of interest income**. Your own note on the
Assumptions tab says exactly this. Across all three years, per-payment fees are the single largest cost
line in the business - larger than the entire payroll in every one of them.

The pricing underneath all of it lapsed on **31 March 2026**. Every cost line in the model flows from a
proposal that is no longer live, and the negotiating position is not strong: the £180k implementation
fee is paid and the Letter of Commitment was signed on 15 May. Your own risk register has this as HIGH
and unresolved.

So the two variables that decide whether this business is worth anything - the Bank of England base
rate and ClearBank's price list - both sit outside management's control.

---

## The activity problem is real, but it is a launch problem

Dream Bank earns on balances sitting still and pays ClearBank when accounts get used.

At Year 1 assumptions a business account does 30 FPS and 2 BACS payments a month, which costs
**£34.56 a year**, and holds £600, which earns **£20.40 of interest plus £9.60 of interchange, so £30**.
The account is loss-making on the ClearBank line before a single overhead is added. Your own unit
economics say the same thing more bluntly: Year 1 revenue per account £34, ClearBank cost per account
£47.

This is not permanent. By Year 3 transaction pricing tiers down to £0.05 and the assumed business
balance is £3,000, at which point the same account costs about £19 in payments and earns £102 in
interest. The contradiction dissolves - but only if average balances rise fivefold, which is itself one
of the plan's larger assumptions rather than an outcome of it. The honest version of this point is that
the model is loss-making per account exactly while it is smallest and least able to absorb it.

---

## The acquisition forecast has no cost in it

This is the assumption we would want addressed before anything else, and it is the one the model never
states.

Marketing is **£3,000 a month pre-launch and £5,000 a month thereafter**. That is £87k of spend to open
50,000 accounts in Year 1, or **£1.74 per account**, and £207k to reach 250,000 accounts by Year 3, or
**£0.83 per account**. UK challenger banks have generally reported acquisition costs in the tens of
pounds, and incumbents in the hundreds. Monzo at its most viral is the closest comparison anyone
reaches and it is still several times this. We are not aware of a bank that has acquired at £1.74, and
the model does not claim one - it simply does not price the channel at all.

What closes the gap is Bear and Simon posting about it. We think that is genuinely the strongest thing
here: distribution is the hardest input in fintech to manufacture and you have it. But in the model as
written it is unpriced, uncommitted and unhedged. There is no cost line, no term, no minimum and no
budget to buy growth if the posts underdeliver. If that promotion slows in eighteen months the plan
does not lose a marketing channel, it loses its acquisition engine and has no second one.

That matters commercially as well as analytically. The proposal that came with the model asks BGV for
an initial £100k into the founding round, while the forecast it is attached to depends on Bear
supplying the distribution for nothing. Those two things need to be reconciled before we can look at a
number. We are not saying the promotion should be expensive - we are saying it should be priced,
because right now it is the single largest input in the plan and the only one with no line against it.

---

## Four things we would want closed

**The round does not cover its own requirement.** Your Capital Requirements tab calculates £3.96m
against a planned raise of £3.5m - **0.88x cover in the base case**. The stress case needs £5.19m,
which is **0.67x**. We would flag that your own risk row describes the stress case as "roughly £4.5m
... 0.78x cover", which does not match the £5.19m the same sheet computes. Either way the base case is
short by £461k before anything goes wrong.

**A cash obligation is missing.** The signed Letter of Commitment requires a Mandated Minimum Balance
under the CCT. No figure for it appears anywhere in the model, and your own register says so.

**A third of the profit comes from products that do not exist.** Ten of them, insurance through lending
to accounting, none built and no partners signed. They carry **£3.86m of Year 3 EBITDA out of £11.5m**.
More pointedly, strip them out and Year 2 goes from £842k of EBITDA to a **loss of £82k**, so they are
not upside on top of a working core, they are what makes the middle year work.

**There is no price.** No valuation, no percentage and no equity appears anywhere in the model or the
covering email. A cheque size has been proposed without saying what it is worth or what it buys.

---

## On rates, one correction to how this is usually framed

The MPC held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 30 July on a 6-3 vote, and the three dissenters voted to **raise**
to 4%, up from two dissenters in June, with inflation forecast to peak around 3.2% in Q4. So the
near-term risk to your interest line is currently skewed upward rather than downward, which is helpful
to you and which your Sensitivity tab already models at +£2.0m to Year 3 EBITDA.

That does not remove the exposure. Interest is 69% of Year 3 combined revenue, a 100bp cut takes about
**£4.0m off Year 3 EBITDA**, and the direction of travel over a four-year forecast is nobody's to
predict. The point is that this is the largest unhedged position in the business and there is currently
no fee, no hedge and no second revenue line standing behind it.

---

## Where we land

The gap here is stage, not honesty. This is a credible team with a real mission and distribution most
fintechs would pay a fortune for, holding a plan that has not finished being built. No valuation, no
current supplier pricing, a round that is short of its own number, and an acquisition forecast resting
on promotion that nobody has priced or committed.

Every one of those is fixable, and none of them needs anything but time and work. We would rather see
them fixed than overlooked, and we would want to look again once they are.

The most useful next step from our side would be a short call on two things specifically: what the
£100k is buying and in what entity, and how the Bear and Simon distribution is going to be treated -
priced, committed, or both.
