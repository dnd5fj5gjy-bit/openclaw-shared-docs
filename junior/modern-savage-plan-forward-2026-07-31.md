## 1. What Modern Savage is

A 100% family-owned supplement brand created and owned by Bear Grylls and his family. Not a partnership, not an endorsement, not a collaboration with someone else's company. That single fact is the commercial asset, because it is the one thing no competitor in this category can copy or buy.

The product is one scoop a day, 23.5g, of the things people used to eat and mostly stopped: grass-fed organs, whole-food vitamins, gut-friendly protein and eight real berries. One pouch is twenty-eight days. It is not a protein shake and it is not a multivitamin.

## 2. What we are trying to do

**The immediate objective:** convert an owned audience that already trusts Bear into a subscription base, before spending anything on paid acquisition.

That framing matters because it decides everything else. This is not a retail product business that happens to sell online. It is a subscription business, and subscription businesses are won or lost on two numbers: how many people start, and how long they stay. Everything in section 3 follows from that.

**The three things that have to be true for it to work:**

1. **The audience converts.** Bear's list and following are the entire acquisition strategy for phase one. If they convert, the business funds itself. If they do not, we find that out now, cheaply, rather than after committing to paid media.
2. **They stay past month two.** The subscription allows cancellation any time after the second delivery, so month three is the real test. A subscriber who reaches month four is worth roughly four times one who does not.
3. **Supply keeps up.** The failure mode that kills subscription businesses is not weak demand, it is selling more subscriptions than you can keep supplied and then stocking out on the people who already paid. That is a planning constraint, covered in section 5.

**The longer objective:** three SKUs in one household. The bundle at £46.66 is not a discount, it is the entire strategy in one line. A household on all three is worth 2.5 times a household on one, costs almost the same to acquire, and is materially harder to cancel because it has become the family's routine rather than a product they bought.

**The thing worth seeing in that table:** on subscription, once VAT is stripped out, the two markets are priced within 1% of each other. £45.83 net converts to $59.58 against a US price of $59.99. ### 3.3 One thing in the model that is now out of date

The model was built for a US-first launch in September with the UK following in November on a separate manufacturing track. The live site says something different: made in the UK, pre-orders open now, delivering around October, with UK and US delivery from UK-made stock.

The unit economics and the per-subscriber maths are unaffected, because they are driven by price, cost and churn rather than by geography. **What does change is the phasing of when subscribers arrive and when cash is committed**, and the funding curve should be re-cut against the actual pre-order and production dates. That is a half-day of work once I have the run size and the production schedule, and it is item 2 in section 6.

### The cash shape of a subscription pre-order

Charging on order rather than dispatch, which is what the site does today, is the right call for cash and it is worth being explicit about why. Money arrives in August and September against product delivered in October. That funds the production run rather than requiring it to be funded and then recovered.

The obligation that comes with it is real and non-negotiable: the card is charged today, so if October slips we owe every one of those customers a clear email with a new date and a working one-click refund, before the date passes rather than after. Getting that wrong on a Bear-fronted brand is a story, not an admin problem. It costs nothing to do properly and it has to be built before it is needed.

## 4. The plan, next 90 days

**August — prove the conversion**

The only question that matters this month is what percentage of the owned audience buys. Run the launch properly to the full list, then read the answer honestly. Conversion on an owned list of this size is the number that tells us whether this is a £400k business or a £2m one, and we will know it within about ten days of the broadcast.

Track three things and nothing else: conversion rate, the split between single and full stack, and the value of the average order. If the bundle is not taking a meaningful share, the page ordering or the pricing gap is wrong and it is cheap to fix in August.

**September — the Summit**

Bear is on the Health Optimisation Summit stage on 12 September in London. It is the single most valuable marketing moment the brand gets this year and it is free. It is also the right room, a UK audience already paying for health, in the market where we now manufacture.

Whatever the pre-order has done by then determines the ask from the stage. Design that moment in August with the number known, rather than writing it the week before.

**October — deliver, and hold them**

Delivery is the promise the entire pre-order was sold on, so it is the only thing that matters in October. Then immediately: the retention problem starts the day the first pouch lands. The second delivery is where cancellation becomes possible, so the month between first and second delivery is where the business is actually won.

That means the onboarding sequence, the recipes, the how-to-use content and the reorder reminders need to exist in September, not be written in November when the churn shows up in the numbers.

**Running through all three: supply.**

Set the reorder trigger by date, not by watching stock. With a manufacturing lead time of roughly eight weeks, run two has to be ordered while run one still looks comfortable. Every subscription business that stocks out does it the same way, by waiting until stock looks low, which is already too late by the length of the lead time.

## 5. The three things most likely to go wrong

**Selling more subscriptions than we can supply.** A pre-order sold in August is not one sale, it is a delivery every 28 days indefinitely. Cover has to outlast the reorder cycle. If the first run cannot supply the subscribers it sold through to run two arriving, we stock out on people who have already paid, which is the most expensive kind of churn there is because it is also a refund and a complaint.

**Missing October.** The card is charged. The obligation is real. Decide now what the trigger is for telling customers early rather than hoping, and write that email in advance.

**NMN in the Summit Stack, sold in the UK.** This is a genuine regulatory flag rather than a hypothetical. NMN's status as a novel food in Great Britain is not settled, and it is currently on sale on a Bear-fronted site. It needs a straight answer from someone qualified, and the answer changes the product rather than the paperwork. Worth resolving before volume rather than after.
