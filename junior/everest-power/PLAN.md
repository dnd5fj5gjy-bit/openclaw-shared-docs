# Bear Grylls Everest Power
## The plan, and the four things I would change about it

Junior, 6 August 2026

---

## The verdict

The idea works. Wholesale-only electrolyte sachets, made in India, sold through a
distributor network you can see into, is a good business and it fits your name better
than almost anything else you could put in an Indian shop. At a Rs 10 sachet we keep
about Rs 3.75, which is a 56% contribution margin, and a distributor running 300
outlets earns about Rs 52,000 a month gross. Both of those numbers are healthy enough
to build on.

Four things in the brief as written will break it, and all four have a better answer.

**One: drop the crypto.** Paying for goods with stablecoins is prohibited in India,
the RBI is actively hostile to private stablecoins, and every transfer carries a 1%
TDS on top of a 30% tax on gains. A kirana owner in Nashik is not opening a wallet.
The thing you actually want, which is seeing every sale and never being cheated, is
already solved better and free by UPI. More on this below, because it is the most
important paragraph in the document.

**Two: no caffeine.** Since 22 September 2025 caffeinated and energy drinks sit in
India's 40% GST slab. Electrolyte powder without caffeine sits at 18% or lower. FSSAI
has also banned the words "energy drink" outright and forces a "not recommended for
children" warning on anything caffeinated. Caffeine costs us 22 points of tax and the
entire under-18 market to gain nothing we cannot get from glucose.

**Three: Himalayan salt is not available to us.** The Himalayan pink salt on every
shelf in the world is mined at Khewra in Pakistan, and India banned all imports from
Pakistan, direct and indirect, on 2 May 2025. We cannot legally put it in an
India-made product. There is a genuinely better answer: real Indian Himalayan rock
salt is mined at Drang and Gumma in Mandi, Himachal Pradesh, and it is the only rock
salt deposit in India. Smaller story, but it is ours and it is true.

**Four: name it Bear Grylls Everest Power, not Everest Power.** Everest is India's
number one spice brand, Rs 2,601 crore of revenue, stocked in 400,000 outlets across
1,000 towns. That is the exact channel we are targeting. Everest alone is a fight we
would lose and do not need.

---

## What the product is

A 12g sachet of glucose and electrolyte powder that makes 300-500ml of citrus drink.
No caffeine, no carbonation, no cold chain, no refrigeration. Shelf-stable for 18
months in 45 degree heat, because that is what a petrol pump in Rajasthan actually is.
Sold in a box of 100 to the shop, sold one at a time to the man drinking it.

MRP Rs 10. That is the number. Ten rupees is India's impulse price point, it is what a
shampoo sachet and a biscuit packet cost, and it is the price at which a customer does
not do arithmetic. Electral sells 21.8g for Rs 23 and Enerzal Zero sells 5g for Rs
14.50, so we are underneath both of them on ticket price while being a bigger serve
than Enerzal.

The functional formula is not clever and does not need to be: dextrose, sodium
chloride, potassium chloride, sodium citrate, a lemon or nimbu-masala acidulant
system, and a small vitamin load. This is a 60-year-old formulation. What we are
selling is the name on the front and a distributor who actually turns up.

---

## Why your name works here specifically

This is the part of the idea I would not change at all. India is not a market where
Bear Grylls is a foreign celebrity with some awareness. Man vs Wild ran on Discovery
across India for a decade, and the episode with Modi is one of the most-watched
factual broadcasts in Indian television history. There is no other health or hydration
brand in India that can put a face on the front of a Rs 10 sachet that a rickshaw
driver in Indore already trusts about survival, heat, and water.

Electral is a pharmacy product. Enerzal is a hospital product. Glucon-D is a
children's product. None of them own the idea of the person who does hard things in
hard heat. We do, and it is on the packet.

## The payment layer, which is where the real idea is

You are right about the problem. Indian FMCG distribution leaks. Distributors
under-report, divert stock across territories, sell into markets they do not hold, and
the brand finds out months later from a sales figure that does not match a shipment.
Your instinct to make every sale visible at the point it happens is the correct
instinct and it is the thing worth building.

You have the right mechanism and the wrong rail.

**Keep:** the app, the barcode scan at the point of sale, digital settlement, one
unique code per box so we know which distributor sold which box into which shop.

**Change:** settle in UPI, not stablecoins. UPI processed 228 billion transactions
worth about $3.6 trillion in 2025. It is instant, it is free, it is already on the
phone of the shopkeeper we are selling to, and it produces exactly the audit trail you
want. Crypto gives us worse traceability than UPI, plus a legal problem, plus a tax
problem, plus an adoption problem.

Here is what the app does. The distributor's rep scans the box code in the shop. The
shop pays by UPI against that scan. The payment confirms in two seconds and the box is
now permanently tied to a GPS point, a shop, a distributor and a timestamp. We can see
sell-in and sell-through on the same screen, in real time, nationally. Diversion
becomes visible the moment a box registered to Pune gets scanned in Surat.

That is a better anti-fraud system than crypto would have given us, and it is a system
Indian shopkeepers already know how to use.

The one thing worth keeping an eye on: RBI is running the Digital Rupee as a CBDC and
it is legal tender with zero fees. If a state-backed programmable rupee matures, that
is where the crypto-shaped idea comes back, legally. Build the app so the settlement
layer can be swapped.

## The unit economics

At MRP Rs 10, 18% GST, no caffeine:

The consumer pays Rs 10. Net of GST that is Rs 8.47. The shop buys at Rs 7.20 and
keeps Rs 1.27, which is 15%, the top of the packaged-FMCG band and deliberately
generous because we are buying shelf space we have not earned yet. The distributor
buys at Rs 6.63 and keeps Rs 0.58, which is 8%, also top of band. We receive Rs 6.63,
pay Rs 2.10 of COGS, Rs 0.33 of brand royalty, Rs 0.15 of freight and Rs 0.30 of trade
spend, and keep Rs 3.75. That is 56.5%.

A box of 100 earns the shop Rs 127, the distributor Rs 58, and us Rs 375.

**Distributor viability, which is the number that decides whether anyone signs up:**
150 outlets doing 2 boxes a month is Rs 17,000 gross, which is not a business. 300
outlets at 3 boxes is Rs 52,000, which is a real job. 500 outlets at 4 boxes is Rs
115,000, which is a career. So the distributor territory has to be drawn at 300 outlets
minimum. Any smaller and we are recruiting people who will quit in four months.

**The load-bearing assumption is 3 boxes per outlet per month**, which is 10 sachets a
day out of one shop. Every scale figure in the model rests on it. It is unproven and I
would not put it in front of an investor until a pilot has either confirmed or killed
it.

If it holds: 60,000 outlets across five states is 18m sachets a month and about £7.6m
a year of contribution. 400,000 outlets national is about £51m. Those are the prize
numbers and they are the ones to treat with the most suspicion.

## GST classification is worth up to 40 points

This is the single highest-value piece of work in the whole plan and it costs a few
thousand pounds.

- Classified as ORS under HSN 3004: 0% GST, we receive Rs 7.82, 62% margin.
- Classified as proprietary food under HSN 2106: 18%, we receive Rs 6.63, 56% margin.
- Classified as a caffeinated beverage: 40%, we receive Rs 5.59, 49% margin.

Same powder, three answers, a 13-point spread. Get an advance ruling from the Indian
tax authority before the formula is locked, and design the formula to land where we
want it rather than discovering afterwards where it landed. I would model the base
case at 18% and treat anything better as upside.

---

## Manufacturing

Make it in India, as you said. India is already the world's largest exporter of
electrolyte powder, so the capability is deep and cheap and we are not doing anyone a
favour by choosing it.

Route: third-party contract manufacture, not our own plant. There are established ORS
and sachet manufacturers with FSSAI licences, existing form-fill-seal lines and spare
capacity. We bring the formula, the artwork and the volume. We do not bring capex.
Site in the west or centre, near a port and near the freight corridors, so we are not
paying to move powder across the country before we start.

What we need from them before anything is real: a quoted price per sachet at 1m, 10m
and 100m units, their FSSAI licence number, a stability trial at 45 degrees and 75%
humidity, and confirmation they can print a unique 2D code per box on the line. That
last one is not standard and it is the piece the whole app depends on, so ask it first,
not last.

## What I would do in the next 90 days

1. **Trademark search and filing** on Bear Grylls Everest Power in classes 5, 30 and
   32, before another word is spoken to anyone in India. Given the Everest situation
   this is the first call, not a formality.
2. **Confirm who licenses the Bear Grylls mark for consumer products** and on what
   terms. The 5% royalty in my model is a placeholder and the real number changes the
   economics.
3. **Three manufacturer quotes** against a written spec, with the per-box unique code
   requirement stated up front.
4. **An advance GST ruling** on the intended formula.
5. **Pick one state and one city for the pilot.** Not five. One distributor, 300
   outlets, 90 days, and the only question we are answering is whether a shop sells 10
   a day.
6. **Build the app thin.** Scan, UPI collect, map, dashboard. Nothing else. The app is
   an anti-fraud instrument, not a product.

The pilot is the whole plan. Everything above it is preparation and everything below
it is scale.

---

## What I am least sure about

The 3 boxes per outlet per month figure, as above. The COGS build, which is my
estimate from trade prices and not a quote. And whether a Rs 10 sachet can carry a
distributor network at all, because Rs 58 a box is thin and the reason FMCG
distributors survive on it is that they carry twenty brands in the same van. A
single-brand distributor at 8% may not be viable, in which case we either widen the
margin or accept that our distributor is someone who already distributes something
else. I lean towards the second.
