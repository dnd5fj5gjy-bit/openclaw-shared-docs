## The short version

Ryan's document runs to six points against the execution draft you sent on 29 July. What is not in it matters more than what is. He has not touched the 45/55 split, the £45 reference price, the 10% royalty, weekly settlement, delivery charges sitting outside the split, the insert, the stockist controls, the 24-month term, the six-month long-stop, or clause 13.1 making him the Responsible Person. The commercial deal you and he agreed between 20 and 23 July is agreed.

He is also holding his solicitor back until after your weekend catch-up, because "the solicitor will want the final version". So the weekend conversation is where this gets settled in principle, and it then goes to a lawyer once. Agree the shape, do not draft live.

Of the six, **two you should give him outright, three you trade, and one you should refuse.** There is also one point where he is asking for less than he is entitled to, and you should offer him more.

## Where he is asking for less than he could have

**Point 1, the liability cap.** He wants the uncapped indemnity in 19.3 brought inside the £250,000 cap in 19.6, because "a business of our size is not able to accept" the exposure.

Clause 14.1 already requires him to carry **£5 million of product liability cover per claim** plus recall cover, for the term and six years after, with our interest noted on the certificate. He is asking to cap his liability at a number **twenty times below the insurance he is already paying for.** If you agreed £250,000, you would be leaving £4.75m of cover on the table that his insurer, not Ryan, would have paid.

So give him the cap and set it at the insurance. Say: total liability capped, including the indemnities, at £5m per claim, or the amount actually recoverable under the policy, whichever is greater, provided the policy in 14.1 is maintained and the certificate produced. He gets the certainty he actually needs, which is that a claim cannot end his company. You get twenty times the protection you would have had if you had simply said yes. And it makes maintaining the insurance the thing his cap depends on, which is worth more than the clause itself.

The second limb of his point 1, that we indemnify him for the Bear Grylls IP and the credit line, is fair and you should take it mutually. But see the last section before you do.

## What to give him

**Point 6, natural variation, is his best point and it is correct.** A 100% natural honey and propolis balm with no stabilisers will soften in summer and firm in winter. Under the draft as written, every seasonal texture change is technically a breach of the 12.2 warranty. That is a fight waiting for the first heatwave.

Agree the principle: an agreed tolerance for texture, firmness, appearance and colour goes into the Schedule 1 specification, and variation inside it is not a defect.

**But refuse the second half of his point.** He wants returns caused by that variation treated as customer change of mind under 11.4 rather than product fault under 11.3. Those are not the same money. Under 11.3 he credits us in full including outbound delivery. Under 11.4 we simply lose the sale, around £16.88 of margin on every unit at the reference price. He is asking us to fund the returns caused by a characteristic of his product.

The answer is his own principle from 22 July. He argued the price should be fixed against RRP, and we settled instead on both sides taking a percentage of what actually comes in, so that a discount is shared in proportion. Clause 6.4 says exactly that. A variation return is the same situation: shared 45/55, in the same proportion as the sale. Neither of us carries it alone.

And ask what "we manage across the seasons" means in practice. If the answer is that he holds shipments or packs differently above a certain temperature, that belongs in the agreement as a shipping protocol, because that is the thing that actually prevents the return. His offer to put an explanation on the listings should be an obligation on both channels, not a courtesy.

**Point 4, third limb.** He wants confirmation that if we step in and self-supply during a stockout, that does not extend to his formulation. Clause 18.3 already says he owns it. Say yes immediately, it costs nothing.

## What to trade

**Point 3, exclusivity.** He is right that 16.1(b) is wide. It stops him selling any honey-and-propolis balm in his own independent range for the 24-month term plus a further 12 months, which is up to three years out of a category, and that is at the aggressive end for a supplier's own range.

Cut the tail, hold the term. Offer three months post-term instead of twelve. Refuse his actual request, which is to narrow 16.1(b) to co-branded products only, because that would let him launch his own honey balm alongside ours on day one. His own argument from 22 July kills it: he pushed the price to £45 precisely so the two products would not compete with each other, because "a customer choosing between the two isn't a customer we've gained, we've just split one sale". That was true then and it is true now.

Remind him 16.3 already stops us launching a competing tallow product. The exclusivity runs both ways.

**Point 4, first and second limbs.** He wants a minimum commitment from us to match his obligation to prioritise our channel, and he wants the ten-day step-in right disapplied for supply-chain delays.

Refuse the minimum commitment. No minimum in either direction is clause 6.2 and it is the reason we chose dropship over his original wholesale proposal. A firm order commitment turns it back into wholesale and puts the stock risk on us.

Refuse the supply-delay carve-out too, but for a specific reason worth saying out loud: every stockout is a supply delay, so that carve-out would swallow the clause. The step-in does not punish him, it only suspends exclusivity so customers can still buy something.

Then give him the thing he actually wants, which is not to be blindsided. Offer a hard obligation on us: fourteen days' notice of any Bear post or campaign expected to spike demand, and if we fail to give it, the step-in right does not apply to the stockout that follows. That answers his real worry, costs nothing, and is a better protection than the minimum he asked for.

**Point 5, approvals.** Hold clause 5.3. "For any reason or none" is the whole architecture of your position on Bear's name, and a reasonableness standard invites an argument about whether each refusal was reasonable, which is exactly what you said you did not want on 20 July.

Give him the second half. Where we withdraw an approval under 5.6 that he has already spent money on in good faith, he takes the material down but is not out of pocket for materials irrevocably committed to that specific approved use. 5.6 already says he does not have to recall printed packs, so this is the same principle extended. Cap it at a modest figure, say £5,000 per withdrawal. Offer to cut the Schedule 2 response from five business days to three for routine trade and stockist uses. Both are cheap and they buy the discretion staying absolute.

## What to refuse

**Point 2, reputation termination.** He wants sell-through, compensation and a remedy period on clause 17.4.

Refuse the remedy period outright. A reputation clause that makes you wait while the other side fixes it is not a reputation clause, it is a notice period, and the entire reason it exists is Bear.

His underlying worry is legitimate though, and it is narrower than he has drafted it: he is worried about being terminated for something that is nothing to do with him, having already bought packaging. Split the clause by cause rather than by consequence. Where the trigger is his act, omission or breach, the position stays exactly as drafted, no sell-through and no compensation. Where it is not, do not give sell-through, because in the one situation where you need to invoke 17.4 you want Bear's name off the shelf that week, not selling for another 90 days. Offer instead to take the committed, unrecoverable, evidenced cost of printed packaging and tooling at cost, capped at a stated figure. He is protected, and you keep the ability to stop it dead.

## Two things on our side of the table

**We are the ones who cannot yet fully answer point 1.** His request for an indemnity on the Bear Grylls IP sits on top of clause 19.2, where we already warrant that we hold every right and written authority needed, including from the owner of the Bear Grylls IP and from any family member whose name is in the credit line, and that the partnership is substantiable under the CAP Code. Turning that warranty into an indemnity does not add much legal exposure, because we have already promised it. What it does is make it expensive if the paperwork is not there. Worth confirming what we hold in writing before you agree the indemnity, not after.

The same question sits under clause 18.2, where BGV grants Fat Cow a licence to use the Modern Savage marks. Modern Savage Inc in Delaware owns that brand, and there is still no intra-group licence between the two.

**The parties clause names Bear Grylls Ventures LLP.** This morning Raemy told Jack Crumpton at SCEND that he would rather contract through Modern Savage Limited, which is being incorporated next week. Two UK entities for the same brand in the same week. Whatever gets decided for SCEND should be decided here too, before this is signed rather than after, because moving the counterparty later means a novation and Ryan's solicitor asking why.
