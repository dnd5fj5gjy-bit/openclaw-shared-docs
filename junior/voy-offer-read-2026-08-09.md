# Voy's offer: what it actually says

**8 August 2026. Junior. For Jesse, ahead of Monday.**

## The verdict

It is a good deal wearing a much bigger number than it has. Take it seriously, and negotiate it as
a £1M deal with options attached, not an £8M one.

The headline is £13.6M. The contracted figure is £8M. The guaranteed figure is £1M. Those are three
different numbers and the gap between them is the whole negotiation.

The £13.6M in the slides is not compensation. It is £5.5M of cash, plus £2.5M of granted equity that
Voy has grown to £7.1M using its own assumed rise in its own valuation. Their footnote admits it:
"estimated equity value at the end of the 4-year partnership period." Strip the appreciation and the
offer is the £8M in the letter. Then strip the milestones, which are contingent, and the years two and
three cash, which Voy can walk away from, and Bear is guaranteed £1M.

That is the real starting point, and it is still a serious offer. But nobody should walk into Monday
anchored on thirteen.

## The clause that matters more than the money

Everything in CJ's summary is accurate. The thing that is not in it is on the last page of the offer
letter, under Usage and approvals, and it is the most important paragraph in the document:

> "Bear will not partner with a competing brand in the health category, inclusive of telemedicine
> providers, online pharmacies, and supplements providers without the prior approval by Voy. While
> pre-existing partnerships are excluded from this, **Bear must not promote blood testing, testosterone
> replacement therapy, or testosterone in any form through any existing or new partnership unless
> approved by Voy.**"

Read the second sentence twice. The pre-existing carve-out protects existing deals in general, and then
is expressly switched off for blood testing, TRT and testosterone. Ted's Health is a UK TRT business
that Bear majority owns. As drafted, this deal takes away his right to promote his own company, for
four years, unless the counterparty gives permission.

CJ's Part 2, integrating Ted's into a broader Voy partnership, is therefore not the upside on top. It
is the repair. Signed as written, the ambassador deal disarms the asset before the conversation about
that asset has started, and it hands Voy the reason it no longer has to pay much for it. If Voy wants
Bear's voice out of the TRT market, that is a thing they are buying, and it should be priced, not
included.

Modern Savage has the same problem in a smaller form. "Supplements providers" is in the restricted
list. Modern Savage has not launched, so whether it counts as pre-existing on the signature date is
genuinely arguable, and nobody should want to argue it later. Both businesses need naming in a written
carve-out, not covered by a general exclusion.

## The other four things I would change

**Voy can leave after a year, Bear cannot.** The opt-out at the 1-year mark on 30 days' notice is
theirs alone. So year one is guaranteed and years two and three are Voy's option. Either make the break
mutual, or price it: a termination fee, or bring the year two and three cash forward, or accept a
one-year term at a higher annual number and let them re-sign. What Bear should not do is give up
category freedom for four years in exchange for a fee that has a twelve month floor.

**Year four pays whatever Voy decides to spend.** There is no fee in year four, only 10% of campaign
spend, and Voy controls campaign spend entirely. Their own slide models Scenario 1 as "no campaign
spend in year 4", so they have already put a zero on the page. If Bear is still exclusive in year four,
year four needs a floor, and "campaign spend" needs a definition, because 10% of media featuring Bear
and 10% of all marketing are wildly different numbers.

**£2.5M of equity in what, exactly.** Share class, valuation basis at grant, vesting, what happens on
a sale, tag-along, anti-dilution: none of it is in the document. Half the earn-out is paid in this.
Until those terms exist, treat the equity as worth materially less than face and say so at the table.

**The right to match is quietly expensive.** Voy gets to match any bona fide offer in men's hormonal or
preventative health at the end of the term. That does not just cost Bear a better deal, it stops the
better deal from being made. Serious bidders do not spend money constructing an offer that an incumbent
can copy. Cut it, or cap it at a short window with a fixed deadline.

## The thing that is not a commercial question

The partnership is "contingent upon Bear joining the Voy platform, being assessed as clinically eligible
for TRT... and, if eligible, speaking truthfully to his personal experience as a Voy patient."

Two separate problems. Commercially, a clinical assessment nobody controls is a condition on the entire
agreement, so the document needs to say what happens if he is assessed ineligible: the deal should
survive and the deliverables move to the preventative Do Health side, rather than the whole thing
collapsing or, worse, creating a quiet incentive around the outcome.

Personally, it is not my call and I will say it once. Bear going public as a paid TRT patient is a
decision about his brand, not a term sheet item, and it should be made on its own before the money is
agreed rather than inherited from it.

## Two things to check before Monday

The numbers in the slides are Voy's own annualised net revenue, unaudited, not filed accounts. Someone
should pull the filed accounts before the milestone ladder is treated as a forecast. Worth knowing:
Voy is the rebranded Manual, the men's health brand, so the TRT business Bear is being asked to front
is the same lineage that has competed with Ted's for years.

Their own chart has a labelling problem. It runs 2024, then 2025 at £328M, then "2025 July" at £540M,
then 2026E at £850M. That middle column is almost certainly July 2026. Ask, do not assume, because the
growth rate the whole milestone table rests on changes depending on the answer.

## What I would say on Monday

Lead with the exclusivity carve-out for Ted's and Modern Savage, because it is the only point where
being wrong is expensive and permanent. Then the mutual break. Then the year four floor. The equity
terms and the matching right are worth real money but they are second-round points, and CJ is right
that the framework is a good one to work from.
