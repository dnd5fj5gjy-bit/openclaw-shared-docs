# Dream Bank - Analysis

**1 August 2026.** Based on the financial model sent by Jack Whettingsteel, 31 July.

---

## The verdict

Dream Bank is an early-stage idea being presented as a fundable plan. The idea has real merit and the
distribution behind it is genuinely unusual. But they do not control any of the levers that decide whether
it makes money, and the round is being marketed before the things their own model flags as serious have
been resolved.

It could become big. On the facts as presented it is not yet investable.

---

## The central problem

They are not building a bank. They are renting one.

ClearBank holds the licence, the accounts and the money. Dream Bank owns the app and the brand. That is a
legitimate way to start, and it avoids years of regulatory work, but it means ClearBank is a landlord
rather than a supplier. ClearBank takes a cut of the interest before Dream Bank sees any of it, and then
invoices separately for the platform, for every account, for every payment and for every identity check.
Across the first three years, more than half the interest those customer deposits generate ends up with
ClearBank rather than Dream Bank.

In the first year it is worse than that. ClearBank's payment charges alone are larger than the entire
interest income. The model concedes this in a footnote and moves on.

And the pricing behind all of it expired in March. Every cost in the plan comes from an agreement that is
no longer live. Nobody has renegotiated it, and Dream Bank has no leverage to do so, because the
implementation fee is already paid and the commitment letter is already signed.

So the two things that determine whether this business is worth anything, the Bank of England base rate
and ClearBank's price list, are both entirely outside management's control.

---

## The design contradiction

Dream Bank earns money when balances sit still. It pays ClearBank when accounts get used.

The largest single cost in the plan is per-payment fees, and those scale with activity. So the model's
economics quietly prefer a dormant account holding a large balance over a busy business account doing
what a business account is for. The better this works as a working bank for entrepreneurs, the worse it
performs financially.

That is not a number that can be tuned. It is a contradiction between the product they are selling and the
way they get paid, and nothing in the plan addresses it.

---

## The growth plan is not a plan

An enormous spike in the launch month, then a perfectly straight line for a year.

Straight lines are what a spreadsheet contains when nobody has built a funnel. There is no conversion
rate, no channel mix and no acquisition cost behind them. The marketing budget is a rounding error, tiny
against the number of accounts being promised, which means the plan assumes founder-led organic reach does
essentially all of the work for free.

That may well happen. Simon's audience is real. But there is no money anywhere in the plan to buy growth
if it underdelivers, so the single largest assumption in the business has no fallback behind it.

---

## The things they should have fixed before asking

**The round is short of its own requirement.** Their model calculates what they need and it is more than
they are raising. It is flagged inside the sheet as a high risk against themselves. That means a second
raise is already scheduled, on their timing rather than anyone else's.

**There is a cash obligation missing entirely.** ClearBank requires a minimum balance under an agreement
already signed in May. No figure for it appears anywhere in the model. That is an unknown call on cash
sitting outside the whole raise.

**A third of the profit comes from products that do not exist.** Ten of them, insurance through lending to
accounting, none built, no partners signed, launching from late next year.

**There is no company.** Companies House shows one entity, Simon's advertising agency, wholly controlled by
him. There is no Dream Bank, no DreamFund, no Dream Media. There is nothing to buy a share of.

**And there is no price.** No valuation, no percentage, no mention of equity anywhere in the entire model.
An amount has been asked for without saying what it is worth or what it buys.

---

## What is genuinely good

The distribution is real, and it is the hardest thing in the world to manufacture. Most fintechs die
because nobody hears about them. That is the one problem this team does not have.

The model itself is honest. It labels its own guesses, it raises the hardest questions against itself, and
it openly documents a serious error an earlier version made and corrected. Almost every criticism above is
already flagged somewhere inside their own spreadsheet. That is unusual, and it says something good about
the people doing the work.

Deposit businesses also compound. If this reaches real scale the economics improve sharply, because the
supplier costs tier down while the interest keeps climbing. The version of this that works, works well.

---

## What would have to be true

Four things, all at once.

Deposits reach several hundred million. Interest rates stay roughly where they are for years, when the
Bank has just split six to three and is leaning towards cuts. ClearBank re-prices without taking more,
having every reason to take more. And growth arrives organically, because there is no budget to pay for it.

Each is individually plausible. All four together, over four years, is a demanding ask, and the plan is
only priced for the version where they all hold.

---

## Where this leaves it

The gap here is not honesty, it is stage. This is a warm, credible team with an attractive mission and
genuine reach, holding a plan that has not finished being built. They have no entity, no valuation, no
current supplier pricing, an underfunded round and a growth assumption with no fallback.

Those are all fixable, and none of them requires anything except time and work. But they are being asked
to be overlooked rather than fixed, and that is the part worth being clear-eyed about.
