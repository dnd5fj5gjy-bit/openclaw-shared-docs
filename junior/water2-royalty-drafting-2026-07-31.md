## The commercial case first

Charles has priced the royalty for us. On 30 July he offered to issue Bear a further 5% of Water2, free,
in consideration for terminating the 10% net profit participation. At the £60m valuation he transacted
at, **that is a £3m offer to buy the royalty out**, made for a business he believes is early in its
growth.

Set that against what the royalty pays if the business does what they say it will. Charles and Craig
both talk about a £600m outcome. A consumer business at £600m on a 20x multiple is roughly £30m of net
profit a year, so 10% is about £3m a year. On a more conservative 30x it is around £2m a year.

**At their own stated scale, the royalty pays every year what Charles offered once to end it.** It is
perpetual, it survives a sale, and it binds any buyer. That is why he has negotiated on it for six weeks
and why he was willing to give away a twentieth of his company to be rid of it.

Bear kept the right asset. The remaining job is to make sure the asset is real.

## The trap in last night's email

Bear wrote: "thank you for honouring your word to refine that definition."

The refinement Craig actually put on the table, on 14 July, Article 1.1, is this:

> "Net profit" shall mean operating profit before Depreciation, Bank Interest, Bank Revaluations,
> Realised Currency Gains, Unrealised Currency Gains, Corporation Tax Charge, Deferred Taxation
> movement and Year End Adjustments.

Every item on that list is accounting noise. Not one of them is a lever anybody would pull to suppress
a royalty. Advertising and marketing are not mentioned, so they stay in, deducted in full before Bear's
10% is calculated.

Craig has been explicit that this is deliberate. On 19 July: "you will note that on our management
accounts we always define advertising costs before our contribution profit," and he quoted IRP to argue
that marketing is a true cost of revenue rather than an overhead.

Bear's own diagnosis, 19 July: "if the company can run my royalty to zero through advertising then the
royalty is meaningless. You might not do it but a buyer could."

He is right, and the wording he has just thanked Charles for does not touch it. We are one polite email
away from closing this out on a clause that leaves the original problem exactly where it was.

# Annex: the drafting instruction set

We do not hold the executed second licence agreement in the workspace, so clause numbering below is
indicative and the definitions must be mapped onto the real document before anything is sent. That is
the first thing to ask CJ for.

Three clauses. Together they close the hole without reopening "gross versus net", which is Charles's
stated red line and an argument that is spent.

### 1. Marketing add-back, for royalty purposes only

The point: the company can spend whatever it likes. It just cannot spend Bear's royalty away.

> For the purposes of calculating the Royalty only, and without limiting the Company's discretion as to
> its actual expenditure, aggregate Advertising and Marketing Expenditure in any Financial Year in
> excess of [X]% of Net Revenue for that Financial Year shall be added back to Net Profit.
>
> "Advertising and Marketing Expenditure" means all amounts recognised by the Company in respect of
> advertising, marketing, promotion, sponsorship, influencer and affiliate fees, agency fees, and paid
> media and platform costs, whether classified as cost of sales, cost of revenue, contribution costs or
> operating expenses, and whether incurred with third parties or with any Related Party.

The classification wording matters more than the percentage. Craig's IRP quote is an argument that
marketing belongs above the contribution line rather than in overheads, so a clause that only catches
"operating expenses" catches nothing. Set [X] off their own historical accounts, at or slightly above
what they have actually spent, so it costs them nothing today and only bites on a step change.

### 2. Creditable annual minimum

The point: a floor costs a performer nothing.

> The Company shall pay the Licensor in respect of each Financial Year the greater of (a) the Royalty
> and (b) the Minimum Annual Royalty of £[Y]. Any Minimum Annual Royalty paid shall be credited in full
> against the Royalty otherwise payable in that Financial Year.

Price [Y] off their forecast, not ours. Craig has said the FY25-26 profit share will be "significant"
and that numbers land mid-September. **Do not agree [Y] before those numbers arrive.** Setting the floor
without knowing what the royalty actually pays is the one way to get this clause wrong, and we are
roughly six weeks away from the answer.

### 3. Change of control step-up

The point: take Charles at his word and put the protection where he himself says the risk is.

> On a Change of Control of the Company, at the Licensor's sole election the Royalty shall be calculated
> as [Z]% of Net Revenue in place of 10% of Net Profit, and the Minimum Annual Royalty shall increase to
> £[Y2]. Change of Control includes any sale of the business or assets, any transfer of the Licensed
> Marks, and any grant of a licence or sub-licence of the Licensed Marks to a third party.

Charles's entire defence has been that *he* would never suppress the royalty, and Bear has accepted that
in good faith. A clause that only operates on a change of control therefore costs him nothing while he
owns the business, and he cannot object to it without contradicting his own argument.

The licensing limb is not decoration. If the marks or the business can be licensed out rather than sold,
a step-up that only triggers on a share sale can be walked around.

### Two smaller items, same document

- **Related parties.** Management charges, service fees and any expenditure with a related party should
  be at arm's length for royalty purposes, or added back. Cheap to ask for, standard, and closes an
  obvious route.
- **Audit right.** An annual right to inspect the calculation, with the Company bearing the cost if an
  error above a threshold is found. Without this the other three clauses are unverifiable and we are
  simply trusting the number we are handed.
