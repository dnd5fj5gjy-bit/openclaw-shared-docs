## Why the offered concession does not solve the problem

Craig's proposed drafting (14 Jul, Article 1.1) defines Net Profit as operating profit **before** depreciation, bank interest, revaluations, currency gains, corporation tax, deferred tax and year-end adjustments.

Every single item stripped out is below the line that matters. Advertising and marketing stay in, deducted before the royalty is calculated. Craig confirms this himself: "on our management accounts we always define advertising costs before our contribution profit," and quotes IRP to argue marketing is a true cost of revenue.

So the redefinition removes accounting noise Bear was never worried about, and leaves untouched the one lever that can run the royalty to zero. Bear's 19 July email diagnosed this exactly. The proposed wording does not answer it.

## The three asks that get there without reopening "gross vs net"

**1. Creditable annual minimum.** A fixed sum per year, credited in full against the 10% of net profit. If the business performs, Charles never pays a penny extra. If the royalty is engineered to zero, Bear still gets paid. Price it off *their* forecast, not ours: on their own path to £600m this is trivially covered. Ask for a number they can concede without flinching, and let the credit mechanic do the work.

**2. Marketing add-back cap, for royalty purposes only.** Marketing and advertising spend above an agreed percentage of net revenue is added back when calculating the royalty base. The company can still spend whatever it wants. It just cannot spend Bear's royalty away. This keeps "net profit" as the defined base, which is Charles's red line, and closes the hole anyway.

**3. Change of control step-up.** Charles's own defence is that *he* would never do this. Take him at his word and put the protection where he says the risk is: on a sale or change of control, the royalty converts to a fixed percentage of revenue, or the minimum steps up materially. Costs him nothing while he owns it. He cannot object without contradicting his own argument.
