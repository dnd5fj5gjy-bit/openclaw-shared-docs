## Article 1

### Organ supplements in the UK: what actually matters, and what does not

*Target queries: best organ supplements UK, beef organ supplements UK, grass fed liver supplement UK*

Organ meats have gone from something your grandmother cooked to something sold in capsules, and the
UK market has filled up quickly. Most of what is written about choosing between them is written by the
people selling them, which is not very useful. Here is an honest version.

**Start with what you are actually buying.** Almost every organ supplement is desiccated organ meat:
freeze-dried or air-dried, ground, and put in a capsule. The nutrition survives the process
reasonably well. What separates a good one from a poor one is not clever processing. It is three
things.

**One: how the animal was raised.** Grass-fed and grass-finished matters, and the two are not the
same. "Grass-fed" alone can mean an animal that spent its final months on grain. Liver in particular
concentrates what the animal ate, which is exactly why it is nutrient dense and exactly why sourcing
is not a marketing detail.

**Two: whether they tell you the doses.** A label reading "proprietary blend, 3,000mg" tells you
nothing about how much is liver and how much is the cheapest organ in the mix. If a brand will not
break down its blend, assume there is a reason.

**Three: which organs, and why.** Liver is the most nutrient dense and the most useful single organ,
which is why nearly everyone starts there. Multi-organ blends add kidney, heart and spleen, each
bringing something different. More organs is not automatically better. A well-dosed liver product
beats a multi-organ blend that contains a token amount of six things.

**What matters less than you would think.** Capsule count is not a useful comparison unless you check
the serving size, because a 240-capsule bottle at eight capsules a day lasts thirty days, the same as
a 120-capsule bottle at four. Compare cost per serving, not cost per bottle. Country of origin matters
mostly as a proxy for farming standards rather than in itself.

**The question nobody asks: will you actually take it?** This is the one that decides whether any of
it works. The most common reason an organ supplement stops working is that the bottle ends up at the
back of a cupboard. Six capsules a day is a real commitment, and a large number of people quietly
stop after a month. Before you compare labels, be honest about whether you will still be doing this
in March.

*Modern Savage makes a powdered daily blend rather than capsules, precisely because of that last
point. It is not the cheapest way to buy organ nutrition and we would not claim otherwise. If cost per
serving is your main concern, a straightforward liver capsule will serve you better and we would
rather say so.*

## Article 2

### Capsules or powder? An honest comparison

*Target queries: organ supplement capsules vs powder, beef liver powder vs capsules, best form organ supplement*

Almost the entire organ supplement market is capsules. There is a reason, and it is not that capsules
are better.

**Why the industry chose capsules.** Organ meat has a strong, specific, metallic flavour. It is
genuinely difficult to eat and genuinely difficult to mask. A capsule solves that by removing taste
from the equation entirely. It is also cheaper to produce, easier to ship, and simpler to dose.

Several brands market this as a feature, promising you will not taste a thing. It is worth noticing
what that promise concedes: the best thing they can say about the experience is that you will not have
one.

**Where capsules genuinely win.** They are cheaper per serving, usually by a wide margin. They travel
well. They require no preparation. Dosing is exact. If you want organ nutrition at the lowest cost and
you do not mind swallowing several capsules daily, capsules are the rational choice and nothing below
changes that.

**Where they lose.** Volume, mostly. Getting a meaningful dose of desiccated organ typically means
four to eight capsules a day, every day. That is a lot of swallowing, and it is the single most common
reason people stop.

There is also a ceiling problem. A capsule can hold organ powder and very little else. It cannot
practically carry protein, fruit or anything with bulk. So a capsule brand tends to sell you organs
and then sell you the rest separately, which is how people end up with six bottles.

**Where powder wins.** It removes the swallowing problem, it carries a much larger and more varied
payload, and it slots into a habit you already have rather than creating a new one. If you already
make coffee or a smoothie in the morning, a scoop is nothing. A pill regimen is a new behaviour, and
new behaviours fail.

**Where powder loses.** It costs more, both to make and to buy. It needs water and ten seconds. And
it lives or dies on flavour: a powder that tastes unpleasant is worse than a capsule, because at least
the capsule was honest about being a chore.

**The honest summary.** If you want the cheapest reliable organ nutrition and you will genuinely keep
taking it, buy capsules. If you have tried capsules and stopped, or you know yourself well enough to
predict that you would, the format is the problem and no amount of willpower fixes a format problem.

## Article 3

### Are organ supplements regulated in the UK?

*Target queries: are organ supplements safe UK, organ supplements regulation UK, beef liver supplement safety*

A fair question, and one almost nobody answers properly, so here it is.

**Organ supplements are food, not medicine.** In the UK they are regulated as food supplements. That
means they must be safe, accurately labelled and free from misleading claims, and they are overseen by
the Food Standards Agency and local authority trading standards. It also means they do not go through
anything resembling the approval a medicine does. No trials, no licence, no pre-market authorisation.

**What that means in practice.** The bar is meaningfully lower than most people assume. A company can
produce a food supplement and sell it without anyone having independently verified that the contents
match the label. Enforcement is real but it is largely reactive.

**What a brand is not allowed to say.** UK rules on health claims are strict. A food supplement may
not claim to prevent, treat or cure any disease. Only authorised health claims from the GB register
may be used, and they must be worded as authorised. So a brand may say a product supports normal
immune function if it contains enough of a nutrient with an authorised claim. It may not say the
product boosts immunity, and it certainly may not say it treats anything.

If you see a supplement brand making disease claims, that tells you something about the brand well
beyond that one sentence.

**Vitamin A is the one genuinely worth understanding.** Liver is extraordinarily rich in vitamin A in
its retinol form, which unlike vitamin C is fat-soluble and accumulates in the body. This is a real
consideration rather than a theoretical one, and it is the reason dosage on a liver product is not
something to freestyle.

Two practical points. If you are already taking a multivitamin, you may be getting vitamin A from
both sources without having added it up.

And this one is not our opinion, it is national guidance. **The NHS lists liver and liver products
among the foods to avoid in pregnancy**, in its own words because *"liver and foods containing liver
have high levels of vitamin A, which can be harmful to your baby."* UK government advice goes further
and says women who are pregnant, or thinking about becoming pregnant, should avoid liver and liver
products **and** supplements containing vitamin A, including fish liver oil, unless a GP advises
otherwise.

So if you are pregnant, breastfeeding or trying to conceive, speak to your midwife, GP or pharmacist
before taking any organ supplement, ours included. We would rather lose the sale.

*Sources: [NHS, foods to avoid in pregnancy](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/);
[NHS, vitamin A](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-a/).*

**What to look for in a brand.** Third-party testing, and a brand that will show you the certificate.
Full disclosure of doses rather than a proprietary blend. Clear sourcing. Claims that sound boring,
because boring claims are usually the legal ones.

*This is general information about how UK supplement regulation works, not medical advice. If you have
a health condition, take medication, or are pregnant or breastfeeding, speak to a doctor or pharmacist
before starting any supplement.*
