03  /  The competitive answer

Somebody has already
said the seat is empty.

The quote above is Warner Bros. Discovery's. It comes from the announcement of Naked and Afraid: Aadimanav, the first Indian adaptation of the format, which premiered on Discovery Channel India in June. The strongest third-party validation of this category is a competitor sitting down in the chair. So here is the difference, in their words and ours.
THEIRS
A cast-based broadcast format
Contributors change every series. There is no continuing figure for an audience to follow, the IP sits with the network, the channel owns the distribution, and nothing accumulates between seasons. When the commission ends, the audience does not move anywhere — because it never belonged to a person.
OURS
A person, and a position
One continuing figure across every season. We own the IP, the channel, the audience and the back catalogue. Every episode compounds into the same asset, in eight Indian languages. A format can be cancelled. A position in a market cannot.
This is a position, not a show. Shows come and go. A position compounds, which means nothing is wasted if a single season underperforms — it is still an instalment towards the same thing.

06  /  The mechanic

Five rules. They are the whole format, and they never change.

Spontaneity on camera is only credible if it is disciplined off it. These are the rules he is held to, and the audience is told them in the first two minutes of episode one.
01
He starts with one place
A start point, and nothing after it. No route, no bookings, no shortlist of places we were always going to reach.
02
The person he is with decides
What he does that episode, and where he goes. Not the producer, not him, not us.
03
They choose who he meets next
Every episode ends with a handoff to somebody the last person picked. He cannot pick his own.
04
He says yes
To whatever he is offered, at whatever standard it is offered at. Refusing is only permitted on the safety list.
05
Nobody knows the ending
Not him, not us, not the sponsor. The season stops where the ninth handoff leaves him.
What is planned is the production, not the story. A local producer clears each link roughly forty-eight hours ahead: permissions, access, insurance, a safety brief. That work is real, and the audience never sees a frame of it. What Jesse is handed is genuinely unknown to him. What the unit is walking into is not unknown to the unit.
Why this is cheaper than a route. No peak permits on a two-month clock, no aero-sports clearance near a sensitive border, no single leg the whole season depends on, and no weather window that can cost a summit. When one link falls through we film the next one, because there was never a schedule to break.
Deliberately excluded: whitewater, and any long-form endurance leg. Every episode is a technical discipline with a visual payoff. No filler, and no travel for travel's sake.

08  /  The finale

Summit it. Then fly off it.

He starts the season in the sea at the bottom of India and ends it on the summit of Dzo Jongo, 6,128 m, climbed on foot — then flies off the top and lands in the valley. An Indian peak, climbed and flown by an Indian-funded production. The whole country, bottom to top.

Dzo Jongo is named deliberately. Stok Kangri, the peak most people assume, has been closed since 2020 over glacier depletion and has no confirmed reopening — anyone in the Indian adventure trade knows this, so the deck does not rely on it. Dzo Jongo is open, permitted through mountaineering operators at the Leh tourism office, and can be twinned with Kang Yatse II in a single window. It sits in high-altitude cold desert within the Hemis protected area, filmed under park permission and the standard wildlife fee. That is a different thing from the tiger reserves and core forest zones we rule out on page 14, and we mean that distinction precisely.

09  /  Tone and look

Shot like a film, cut like a channel.

We keep what worked in the format Bear built — the accessibility, the direct address to camera, the jeopardy an audience can read without being told. What we drop are the constraints broadcast imposed, none of which apply here.
THE BIG ONE
Continuation is now an asset.
Broadcast made every episode stand alone, so nothing could build. YouTube rewards the opposite: serialisation compounds watch time and a season arc creates returning subscribers. A continuous journey was structurally impossible on television. It is the optimal shape here.
Small unit, big frame
A five-person travelling crew at Indian rates. Drone and long lens carry the scale; nothing is staged that cannot be done for real.
Titles that need no context
A number, a place, or a first. The BASE exit works as a thumbnail in any market on earth with no cultural translation at all.
Reference frames for grade and framing: desaturated earth palette, available light, the figure kept small so the country stays the co-lead. Nothing shot handheld that could be shot still.

11  /  The ceiling-breaker

One shoot.
Eight Indias.

Every episode is released fully dubbed into India's major languages, in Jesse's own voice, cloned — not a stranger reading his lines. The relationship with the audience survives translation, which is the thing dubbing normally destroys.
EnglishHindiBengaliMarathiTelugu TamilGujaratiKannadaMalayalam
This is why the ceiling exists
India's biggest travel and adventure channels stop near four million partly because they are single-language. Nobody has served all of them at once.
Near-zero marginal cost
One shoot, one edit, one grade. Each additional language is an audio pass, not another production. These economics did not exist three years ago.
What the sponsor is buying
One integration, delivered eight times over in the languages their customers actually think in — plus the English original worldwide, at no extra cost to them.
Handled properly, or not at all
Audiences punish a bad dub harder than no dub. Hindi is quality-controlled by a native speaker before release, the smaller languages roll out only once they clear that bar, and every synthetic track is disclosed. Cloning his own voice with his consent is not a deepfake, and we say so plainly.
Voice-clone quality, platform disclosure rules and per-language reception are all tested on a single pilot episode before the full slate commits to this. It is item three of the next steps.

13  /  The money

Indian brands pay for it,
before a frame is shot.

$250k
A season, all in, paid up front.
That is three to six category sponsors at the top of the current Indian rate card, or one title sponsor priced above it. Naming the shape of the cap table is the first question every brand asks, because it tells them what they are buying and who they are sharing it with.
What the rate card actually says
Integrated sponsorships with the largest Indian YouTube creators run ₹15–80 lakh, roughly $18,000–95,000, and YouTube integrations price at three to five times the equivalent Instagram Reel. A cinematic series delivered in eight Indian languages plus worldwide English prices above that card, not inside it.
Who we are selling to
Indian brands first: two-wheeler and automotive, telecoms, apparel, hydration and nutrition, watches, insurance. Global endemic brands second, as a top-up rather than the base.
Why we keep the ad revenue
An India-only audience earns roughly a tenth of what a UK or US one does per thousand views. Global English viewing is our margin, not our funding. We are not betting the business on ad rates.
Why it costs this little
Two tight shooting blocks, a five-person travelling crew, Indian rates, and no unit ever flying between regions. The route design is the cost control.
Where the risk actually sits, stated honestly. Once a season is funded there is no production risk on our side: if it underperforms, the sponsor has still had their reach and we have still built the position. Until a lead sponsor signs, every pound of risk is ours.

14  /  What could go wrong

Named up front,
not buried at the back.

01
Air disciplines drive the insurance
BASE and high-altitude flight need a specialist broker, named certified operators and a signed rescue plan before a frame is shot. Any brand's legal team will ask, so it is priced in from the start rather than discovered later.
02
Permits are dependencies, not tasks
IMF peak permits, aero-sports clearance in airspace near a sensitive border, Ladakh inner-line permits, state film permissions and drone approvals. Long lead times, in the schedule from day one.
03
Altitude is the schedule risk
A 6,000-metre summit with a flyable weather window is the one leg that cannot be forced. Contingency days and a lower fallback peak, rather than assuming the mountain cooperates.
04
Reputational lines we will not cross
No filming in tiger reserves or core forest zones. There is precedent in Bear's India work and it drew a serious backlash. The upside is small and the cost is pre-loaded, so the line is drawn now and in writing.
A sponsor's legal team reads this page first. Everything on it is a known cost with a known owner, which is a materially different proposition from a production that has not thought about any of it yet.
